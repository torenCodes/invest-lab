# The Fiber Backbone of AI: Why Optical Infrastructure Is the Sleeper Trade of the Decade

*Published March 2026*

---

Everyone knows the AI story. Nvidia prints money. Cloud hyperscalers spend hundreds of billions. Data centers multiply like rabbits. But there's a quieter part of this story that a sharp group of investors started flagging years before it became consensus: the optical buildout.

While the world was fixated on GPUs, a handful of voices on YouTube and X were pointing at something less glamorous but just as important — the cables, transceivers, and photonic components that actually move data between all those chips. They were early. They were right.

---

## Why Optics? Why Now?

Modern AI workloads are fundamentally different from what data centers were originally built for. Training a frontier model means thousands of GPUs working in tight sync, passing enormous amounts of data back and forth constantly. The bottleneck often isn't compute. It's bandwidth.

Fiber solves this in a way copper can't. It moves data as light, which means it covers distance with far less power loss and signal degradation than copper can manage at scale. As AI clusters grew from hundreds to tens of thousands of GPUs, demand for high-speed optical transceivers, coherent modules, and photonic components exploded. It hasn't let up.

The numbers back this up. Hyperscalers like Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta have each committed to multi-hundred-billion-dollar infrastructure plans through 2027. A significant chunk of that flows into optical networking — inside data centers, between data centers, and out into the metro and long-haul networks that AI traffic is now straining in ways nobody planned for.

## The Players Worth Knowing

Several publicly traded companies sit at the center of this buildout, and a few have been on the radar of forward-looking investors for years.

**II-VI / Coherent (COHR)** is the heavyweight. After the merger of II-VI and Coherent Corp, the combined company became one of the most vertically integrated optical component manufacturers in the world, making everything from compound semiconductors to 400G and 800G transceivers to coherent modules. As data center speeds push toward 1.6T, COHR is deeply embedded in that roadmap. The integration story has been messy, but the underlying demand is hard to argue with.

**Lumentum (LITE)** makes precision optical and photonic products, with solid positioning in 3D sensing, telecom, and increasingly data center optics. Their indium phosphide (InP) laser expertise is core to the high-speed transceivers AI clusters depend on. Historically volatile with telecom cycles, but the data center tailwind is a newer and more durable driver than anything they've seen before.

**AXT Inc. (AXTI)** is the picks-and-shovels play beneath the picks-and-shovels. AXT makes the compound semiconductor substrates — indium phosphide, gallium arsenide, germanium — that are the raw material for the lasers and photodetectors inside optical components. When COHR and LITE ramp production, AXTI benefits upstream. Small-cap, higher risk, but tightly tied to optical growth without the integration complexity of the bigger players.

A few more worth keeping tabs on:

**Fabrinet (FN)** is a contract manufacturer for optical components — they assemble the finished transceivers for many of the names above. As optical volume grows, Fabrinet's highly automated Thailand facilities scale right along with it. Clean balance sheet, consistent execution.

**Ciena (CIEN)** operates at the networking systems layer. Their WaveLogic coherent technology powers long-haul and metro networks, and AI is driving traffic growth outside the data center that nobody fully anticipated. Less of a direct AI play than the component names, but catching the same tailwind.

**Applied Optoelectronics (AAOI)** is a smaller, more speculative name in data center transceivers. Higher volatility, but meaningful demand upticks from hyperscaler relationships have started turning heads.

**Marvell Technology (MRVL)** straddles semiconductors and silicon photonics. They're developing custom silicon photonics solutions for hyperscalers that could reshape how optical components get integrated at scale — a larger-cap way to get exposure to the optical story with less pure-play risk.

## Why This Trade Makes Sense

Every GPU cluster needs to be networked. Every data center needs to connect to others. Every byte of AI-generated content travels over fiber. Optical is the connective tissue holding the whole AI era together.

A few things make this interesting from an investment angle:

**Supply constraints are real.** High-speed optical transceivers — especially 400G, 800G, and the emerging 1.6T — require specialized manufacturing, compound semiconductors, and long qualification cycles with hyperscalers. You can't spin this stuff up overnight. Companies already qualified and ramping have a durable advantage over anyone trying to catch up.

**The upgrade cycle keeps coming.** Data center optical infrastructure gets refreshed as speeds increase. The move from 100G to 400G was a multi-year cycle. The move to 800G and beyond is underway right now. Each generation means new transceivers, new switches, new fiber plant. That's recurring demand, not a one-time build.

**Valuation has lagged the narrative.** Nvidia trades at eye-watering multiples. A lot of optical component companies still sit at relatively modest valuations despite real secular tailwinds. As the AI infrastructure theme broadens beyond GPUs into the full stack, there could be room for multiple expansion here.

---

## The Risks Are Real Too

Optical companies are historically cyclical. Telecom spending booms and busts have burned investors before, and inventory digestion cycles can be genuinely painful. Integration execution risk at COHR is still a live concern. And if AI capex ever hits a serious wall, the whole infrastructure trade faces a re-rating.

But the structural case — that AI requires massive, sustained optical buildout — is well-supported by the spending commitments hyperscalers have already locked in through the rest of this decade.

**Yiannis Zourmpanos (@yianisz on X)** posted what may be the clearest synthesis of the photonics moment to date. In a March 11, 2026 thread, he laid out the full supply chain — from transceiver names like LITE, COHR, AAOI, and FN, to DSP/silicon players like MRVL and CRDO, to foundry and equipment names, all the way up to substrate leaders including AXTI, SIQE, and SOI. **Davy (@Blinklebloop on X)**, also on March 11, 2026, highlighted a lesser-known angle: SOITEC, a French company with a near-monopoly on Smart-Cut technology used to make SOI (silicon-on-insulator) wafers — the substrate that goes into photonic integrated circuits (PICs), which go into the transceivers powering AI clusters. Finding the company two levels beneath the headline names is exactly the kind of research that gets rewarded early.

**Martin Shkreli** has been one of the more vocal and unconventional voices here, publishing a detailed Substack essay on photonic computing and taking an advisory role at Q/C Technologies (QCLS). His focus is on the longer-horizon possibility of photonics *replacing* GPUs for matrix multiplication entirely — more speculative than the transceiver buildout discussed here, but part of the same broader conviction that light is the future of computing infrastructure. His willingness to make the argument loudly, regardless of how the mainstream received it, helped put the topic on more investors' radar.

That kind of bottom-up public research — supply chain digging, earnings call analysis, connecting upstream substrates to downstream hyperscaler capex — is how ideas like this travel before they become consensus. If you found this thesis through any of these voices, you were early.

## The Bottom Line

The AI infrastructure trade isn't just about who makes the chips. It's about who makes the light that connects them. Optical component and systems companies — COHR, LITE, AXTI, FN, CIEN, and others — are sitting at the center of a multi-year, multi-hundred-billion-dollar buildout cycle. The story is maturing from early-adopter insight to institutional consensus, but there's likely still runway ahead as speeds increase, clusters grow, and the world's data centers keep transforming into AI factories.

The fiber is being laid. The light is already moving.
